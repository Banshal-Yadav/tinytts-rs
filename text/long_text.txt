The history of speech synthesis is a long and surprisingly stubborn one. For most of the twentieth century, the best a machine could do was imitate a human voice in the crudest possible way, buzzing through a bank of analog filters and hoping the listener would fill in the gaps. The results were intelligible, barely, and they sounded like a robot trapped inside a pipe. Then came concatenative synthesis, which stitched together thousands of tiny recorded fragments, and for a while that was good enough for telephone menus and talking clocks. Neural networks changed the rules entirely. Instead of assembling pieces of recorded speech, a network could learn the mapping from text to waveform directly, and by the early twenty twenties the best systems were nearly indistinguishable from a person reading aloud. The catch was size. A good neural voice often needed hundreds of millions of parameters, a GPU, and a small power station to run in real time. Paradee takes the opposite bet. It asks how small a voice can be before it stops being useful, and it answers with fewer than eight million parameters, a single eight megabyte file, and a decoder that never upsamples. The result is a model that speaks faster than you can listen, on one thread of an ordinary laptop processor, with no graphics card and no network connection. That is the whole point. A voice that runs anywhere is a voice that can live inside a browser tab, a phone, a cheap embedded board, or a program you wrote on a rainy afternoon. It will not win a contest against a giant model trained on a thousand hours of studio recordings, but it does not need to. It needs to be small, fast, and clear, and on all three counts it delivers.
